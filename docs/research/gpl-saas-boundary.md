## Bottom line
Under the GPL-3.0 text itself, running ERPNext, HRMS and Webshop as a hosted service, with no copy of the code given to anyone, is not "conveying". Section 2 lets us run, modify and propagate covered works we do not convey "without conditions", so there is no duty to publish source, show licence notices or license our code under the GPL. The FSF, which wrote the licence, says the same in its FAQ, and so do two posts by a Frappe staff member on Frappe's forum, the first of which carries the disclaimer "I am not a lawyer". This is as settled as a licence-text question gets without a court ruling. One part is not settled by the text: section 2 treats handing copies to hosting providers or contractors as conveying, permitted only on its conditions (rule 4); whether renting cloud servers hands a provider a copy at all is INFERENCE. Frappe Framework itself (v15.126.0) is **MIT**, not GPL; the GPL-3.0 parts are ERPNext, HRMS and Webshop. No court anywhere has ruled on GPL and SaaS, and we found no open-source licence case at all in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt or Qatar. The real exposure is not SaaS. It is (a) **any act that hands over a copy**: engine JS/CSS reaching browsers, on-premises or customer-cloud installs, resellers, mobile bundles, raw engine database dumps, and possibly copies moved between our own group companies; and (b) **our in-process apps**: under the FSF's view they are probably works "based on" ERPNext once they import its Python modules. That costs nothing while we never convey. The moment we do convey, they must go out under the GPL. All four local copyright laws give authors an exclusive "communication to the public" or "making available" right. That does not change the answer, because GPL-3.0 already counts "making available to the public" as propagation, which section 2 allows without conditions when no copy is transferred. The points a local lawyer must still weigh are whether a local court would apply the GPL's own definitions, the statutory written-form rules for licences (the worse outcome is not a source duty but no valid licence, so no right to run the engine at all), intra-group transfers and database dumps. Webshop is GPL-3.0, not AGPL. Frappe's newer apps (CRM, Helpdesk, Insights, Gameplan, LMS, Drive, Print Designer, Books, and Press, the code behind Frappe Cloud) are AGPL-3.0 and stay forbidden.

What this means (INFERENCE):
1. Hosting the engine and letting customers use it over the network is, at most, "communication to the public" or "making available" under these laws. GPL-3.0 already lists "making available to the public" as propagation. Section 2 allows propagation without conditions when no copy goes to another party. The FSF says it coined "propagate" and "convey" "to make our intent as clear as possible no matter where the license is interpreted" (S2 #WhyPropagateAndConvey). So a local court that applies the licence as written reaches the same answer.
2. The written-form rules (UAE Art. 9, KSA Art. 11, Egypt Art. 149, Qatar Art. 8) are aimed at transfers and licences of economic rights. If a court held that an unsigned GPL is not a valid licence, the result would be worse than a source duty: we would have no permission at all, so no right to run, copy or modify the engine, and use "without prior authorization" is an offence in the UAE (Art. 41) and similar provisions exist in the other texts (KSA Art. 21(6), Egypt Art. 181). UAE Art. 12 does not settle this. It opens "Without prejudice to the provisions of Article (9)", so it may keep the written-form rule rather than cure it, and it speaks of terms stated on the package, at installation or on the device screen, so whether a `license.txt` in a source repository counts is doubtful. **Effect: unresolved.** KSA, Egypt and Qatar have no equivalent provision in the texts read.
3. A source-disclosure duty cannot arise from these statutes alone. It can only come from the GPL's conditions, and those conditions are tied to conveying.

## Compliance rules we must keep (testable)
1. **No GPL engine asset reaches a customer device.** The gateway serves no path under `/assets/erpnext/`, `/assets/hrms/`, `/assets/webshop/`, and no Desk or website routes (`/app`, `/desk`, `/login`, web pages). Test: a gateway route allowlist test fetches those paths and expects 404. Frappe Framework assets are MIT but are also blocked (ADR-0016).
2. **Our web, mobile and desktop bundles contain no engine code.** Test: a licence and SBOM scan of every shipped bundle fails on any `frappe`, `erpnext`, `hrms` or `webshop` package or file, and on any GPL or AGPL licence.
3. **No copy of the engine or our in-process apps leaves our control.** No on-prem, customer-controlled cloud, reseller or white-label image, partner sandbox image, or code escrow without a new ADR and counsel sign-off (ADR-0029, LEG-017). Test: contract templates contain no delivery-of-software clause, and the release pipeline publishes images only to our private registry.
4. **Hosting providers and contractors only under the section 2 conditions.** Handing them copies is conveying, which section 2 allows only "for the sole purpose of having them make modifications exclusively for you, or provide you with facilities for running those works, provided that you comply with the terms of this License in conveying all material for which you do not control copyright." So: they work for us, under our direction and control, on terms that forbid copying outside the relationship, and on accounts we own; and for every GPL work we hand them (ERPNext, HRMS, Webshop, and our in-process apps if they are combined works) we comply with the GPL's conveying terms towards them, which means licence notices and the source available to them (INFERENCE as to what compliance requires in each set-up). Test: each infra or contractor agreement has the direction-and-control clause, and the engine image carries the licence texts and a source offer (legal checklist).
5. **Customer exports are business data in our own formats, never engine artefacts.** No raw site backups, `mysqldump` of the engine database, bench folders or app code to customers. A full engine database holds copies of ERPNext DocType definitions, print formats and report definitions (INFERENCE). Test: the export service has a format allowlist (CSV, JSON, XLSX, PDF of our templates) and refuses site-backup jobs for customer requests.
6. **Customer-facing documents use our own templates.** Use our print formats, email templates and letter heads, not stock ERPNext or HRMS ones copied verbatim (#WhatCaseIsOutputGPL; ADR-0016). Test: print and email template names are in our app namespace, and a CI check rejects stock template names on customer routes.
7. **One licence rule: a blocklist plus an allowlist, for apps, pip, npm and containers.** The **blocklist** is licences that fail the build wherever they appear: AGPL-3.0 (any version; its section 13 makes a modified version "prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network ... an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source", S4), and any licence the scanner reports as unknown or NOASSERTION until a register row clears it. It names CRM, Helpdesk, Insights, Gameplan, LMS, Drive, Print Designer, Books, Press and lavaloon `ksa_compliance`. The **allowlist** is the licence register: every Frappe app (`bench list-apps` or `apps.txt`), every pip package in the engine image and in our services, every npm package in our bundles, and every container base image must have a register row, and GPL or LGPL licences are allowed only where that row says so. Today that is ERPNext, HRMS and Webshop (GPL-3.0) inside the engine image, and **MariaDB server (GPL-2.0)** as a separate program reached over a network socket, never linked into our code and never conveyed (FAQ #MereAggregation; the engine's Python driver PyMySQL is MIT, S26). GPL or LGPL in a shipped client bundle is never allowed (rule 2). Test: the image build and each service and web build run a licence scan over the app list, the pip and npm lockfiles and the image layers, and fail on a blocklisted licence or on anything with no register row. Known gap today: psycopg2, which Frappe pins, reports NOASSERTION in the licence API and has no register row (S26).
8. **Every engine-side app has a decided licence.** `app_license` is never "TBD" at G1 (currently TBD in `change_engine/hooks.py`). Test: a CI check reads every `hooks.py` under `apps/engine/` and `components/` and requires a value from an approved list.
9. **Keep in-process apps thin and conveyable.** Proprietary logic (compiler, AI, Blueprint, pricing, prompts) lives in external services over the API. In-process apps hold only adapters, hooks and fixtures we could publish under GPL-3.0 if we ever had to. Test: review checklist item, plus a size and import report of `from erpnext` and `from hrms` per in-process app at each gate.
10. **Re-check licences at every engine bump.** On a version change, re-read the licence files of ERPNext, HRMS and any added app. Frappe moved its newer apps to AGPL, so a future change for ERPNext is possible (INFERENCE). Test: the version-policy step already requires it (engine-version-policy.md); add a licence API diff to the bump PR.
11. **Trademark.** No "Frappe", "ERPNext" or "Frappe Cloud" in our product, company or domain names, logos, ads or keyword buying. Descriptive mention only in contracts and diligence, per the ADR-0029 wording. Test: a brand lint over `web/marketing` and ad copy.
12. **Patent non-aggression.** We do not bring a patent claim alleging that ERPNext or HRMS infringes. Doing so breaks section 10 and puts our licence at risk. Test: legal checklist item before any patent filing or assertion.
13. **One operating entity runs the engine** until counsel answers the intra-group question (#DistributeSubsidiary). Otherwise treat copies moved between group companies as conveying, with GPL compliance for everything moved. Test: the infra register records which legal entity owns each engine host.

## What stays uncertain, bluntly
1. **No court has ruled on GPL plus SaaS, anywhere.** The position rests on the licence text, the FSF's reading and Frappe staff's forum reading. That is strong, because the text is explicit ("Mere interaction ... is not conveying"), but it has not been tested in court.
2. **Whether our in-process apps are derivative works is unsettled law.** The FSF's address-space test has not been adopted by a court. *Hellwig v. VMware*, the closest case, ended on evidence. Frappe has no written policy despite saying it would consider one. This is harmless only while rules 3 to 5 hold, and it becomes binding the day we convey.
3. **No GCC or Egyptian court has applied an open-source licence (in our searches).** We do not know whether a court in Riyadh, Dubai or DIFC/ADGM, Cairo or Doha would apply the GPL's own definitions, treat it as a contract or a licence, enforce it without a signature given the written-form rules, or bring in public-order or Sharia-based rules. The DIFC and ADGM common-law courts were not researched.
4. **Statute texts are unofficial English.** The UAE official portal blocked fetches and the UAE text is a third-party reproduction; Egypt's English text predates later amendments; Qatar amendments were not checked; the Arabic texts govern. The article numbers and quotes above must be checked against official Arabic texts. Under RULES 33 these facts may drive scope only after a lawyer confirms them or both founders record an exception.
5. **Written-form rules: the worse outcome is no licence at all.** If a local court held that an unsigned GPL is not a valid licence, we would have no right to run the engine, not merely a source duty. UAE Art. 12 is "without prejudice to" Art. 9 and may not cure this. Unresolved.
6. **Hosting providers and contractors.** Section 2 treats giving them copies as conveying, allowed only on its conditions (rule 4). Whether renting cloud servers hands over a copy, and whether standard cloud terms meet the direction-and-control condition, is INFERENCE.
7. **Intra-group transfers** (for example a Saudi company hosting for a UAE company) are, by the FSF's own answer, "a matter to be decided ... under the copyright law of the appropriate jurisdiction".
8. **"GPL-3.0-only" or "or later"** for ERPNext, HRMS and Webshop is not stated in what we read. It matters little for SaaS but matters if we ever convey.
9. **Database content.** Whether an engine database dump counts as a copy of ERPNext code (DocType JSON, print formats, Jinja templates, report queries) is our inference, not a known position.
10. **Business risk, not legal risk.** Frappe could license future ERPNext versions under AGPL, as it already does for its newer apps. We could stay on the last GPL version but would lose upstream fixes and tax updates.
